Neocloud Field Guide

Who sells AI compute, who builds and powers the sites, how it gets financed, and what that implies for how Kona should make money.

Research gathered 4 Oct 2026, updated 26 Oct · figures are from company releases and trade press, linked per section · items marked as flagged need checking against filings before use in a model

The market in seven points

  1. Demand is compounding. Google's monthly token volume rose about 7x in the year to May 2026, to 3.2 quadrillion. Anthropic's revenue run-rate went from about $1B at the start of 2025 to a reported pace above $100B in September 2026, though the $16.8B of Q3 revenue it showed investors annualizes to about $67B. The four largest hyperscalers guide to about $725B of capex this year, against $410B in 2025. Google says it must double its token-serving capacity every six months, mostly through software.
  2. Pricing has turned up. Public neoclouds now quote revenue per megawatt. Nebius reports $20–25M per MW per year on 1–3 year contracts and $40–50M on deals under six months. CoreWeave reports about $40M per MW on 3–6 month contracts. SpaceX rents all of Colossus 1 to Anthropic at $1.25B a month, roughly $50M per MW, on 90-day exit terms. H100 rental indices are up about 14% over twelve months.
  3. Anthropic is the marginal buyer of 2026. Its prospectus shows at least $518B of compute obligations over a decade. It signed with CoreWeave, Nscale, Lambda (reported), Volta, Fluidstack, TeraWulf, Riot and Hut 8. Meta is the other large buyer.
  4. Cost of debt follows the tenant, not the hardware. Debt backed by an investment-grade contract prices at SOFR + 225–300 bps. The same GPUs against weaker customers price at SOFR + 500–750 bps, sometimes with lender return floors.
  5. Landlords signed 2026 leases at about $140–200 per kW-month. Development yields run 10–14% on build costs of $9–16M per MW. Only about 15% of the capacity contracted by the listed ex-miners is operating.
  6. Bringing your own power is now mainstream. Goldman projects 67 GW behind the meter by 2030. Turbines are sold out for years, so reciprocating engines and fuel cells carry the near-term load at an all-in cost of roughly $100–165 per MWh in the US.
  7. A gigawatt costs $35–60B, and most of it is chips. Crusoe's CEO puts the site and power plant near $20M per MW and the compute near $40M. NVIDIA says $50–60B, Blackstone $55B, Foxconn about $47B for Vera Rubin, Bernstein about $35B. Renting the chips earns about $15M per MW a year; adding managed inference can lift that toward $30M.

The value chain, from electrons to tokens

Every company in this guide sits on one or more of these layers. The further up you go, the more revenue per megawatt, the more capital per megawatt, and the shorter the contract.

    What Kona is selling against

    Kona's asset is cheap, stranded gas turned into firm power at a site that needs no grid interconnection. Its constraints are scale (tens of megawatts while the market signs hundreds), country risk, and distance from customers.

    The six models below are scored against those facts. The view at the end is a framing for discussion with Matías and Ken. It is not investment advice, and the Kona-specific numbers are arithmetic on market data points, not Kona's own model.

    Fuel cost per MWh is heat rate (8–9 MMBtu per MWh for engines and simple-cycle turbines) times gas price.         How it works

        Capacity = original deal ÷ base revenue per MW ÷ contract years. Capex = capacity × capex per MW. During the contract, revenue = price-adjusted revenue per MW × capacity; after it, the residual percentage of that revenue runs to the end of useful life. Opex = revenue × (1 − EBITDA margin). Debt = debt share × capex, repaid in equal annual amounts over the contract, with interest on the opening balance. Cash tax = max(0, EBITDA − capex ÷ useful life) × tax rate. The unlevered IRR is on capex against EBITDA less cash tax, so the debt lines show the funding and the owner's cash position but do not change the return.

        Reading it for Kona

        The GPU case is a CoreWeave-style contract priced at new-contract rates; it clears 15% only if price-adjusted revenue stays near $13M per MW and capex near $30M per MW, and Kona's all-in cost with its own power is closer to $59M. The hosting and energy cases earn less per MW but on 10-year terms with long useful lives, which is where a landlord's return comes from. Argentine cash tax is 35%, or 25% under RIGI; the base case uses 25%. Illustrative only.

        How to read it

        Delayed-draw loan

        The senior facility is committed at the advance rate on total capex and drawn alongside each construction payment, so interest runs only on what is drawn and a commitment fee runs on the rest. Interest during construction is paid by equity. After any interest-only period it amortizes level, is sculpted to a target debt service cover, or is repaid in one bullet; anything still outstanding at tenor is repaid as a balloon, which is where refinancing risk sits.

        Prepayment

        A customer prepayment is cash in during construction and a rent credit afterwards: headline rent stays the same but cash rent is lower for the contract term. It is the cheapest capital in the market and the hardest to get.

        Term mismatch

        When the contract is shorter than the loan, rent after the term is marked down to the renewal price. The GPU case reproduces CoreWeave's DDTL 5.5: three-year contracts, a five-year loan, chips re-rented at half price in years four and five.

        What it leaves out

        Taxes, Argentine withholding and remittance limits, currency, working capital, GPU refresh capex and any value of the asset at the end of the horizon unless you set a terminal multiple. Treat the outputs as a way to compare structures, not as a forecast.
