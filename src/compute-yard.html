Neocloud Field Guide

Who sells AI compute, who builds and powers the sites, how it gets financed, and what that implies for how Kona should make money.

Research gathered 4 Oct 2026, updated 28 Oct · figures are from company releases and trade press, linked per section · items marked as flagged need checking against filings before use in a model

The market in seven points

  1. Demand is compounding. Google's monthly token volume rose about 7x in the year to May 2026, to 3.2 quadrillion. Anthropic's revenue run-rate went from about $1B at the start of 2025 to a reported pace above $100B in September 2026, though the $16.8B of Q3 revenue it showed investors annualizes to about $67B. The four largest hyperscalers guide to about $725B of capex this year, against $410B in 2025. Google says it must double its token-serving capacity every six months, mostly through software.
  2. Pricing has turned up. Public neoclouds now quote revenue per megawatt. Nebius reports $20–25M per MW per year on 1–3 year contracts and $40–50M on deals under six months. CoreWeave reports about $40M per MW on 3–6 month contracts. SpaceX rents all of Colossus 1 to Anthropic at $1.25B a month, roughly $50M per MW, on 90-day exit terms. H100 rental indices are up about 14% over twelve months.
  3. Anthropic is the marginal buyer of 2026. Its S-1 shows $265.6B of future fixed or minimum compute purchases and $321.4B of leases not yet commenced at June 2026, against $16.3B of first-half revenue; compute cost fell to 55% of sales from 201% a year earlier. It signed with CoreWeave, Nscale, Lambda (reported), Volta, Fluidstack, TeraWulf, Riot and Hut 8. Meta is the other large buyer.
  4. Cost of debt follows the tenant, not the hardware. Debt backed by an investment-grade contract prices at SOFR + 225–300 bps. The same GPUs against weaker customers price at SOFR + 500–750 bps, sometimes with lender return floors.
  5. Landlords signed 2026 leases at about $140–200 per kW-month. Development yields run 10–14% on build costs of $9–16M per MW. Only about 15% of the capacity contracted by the listed ex-miners is operating.
  6. Power equipment, not chips, sets the schedule. Of $100 of AI capex, $20 is power and $50 is chips (BNP), but engines take 18–36 months, transformers 17–35 and medium-voltage switchgear 12–24, while a GB200 rack ships in weeks. Behind-the-meter OEMs are about two-thirds booked for 2028. A 24-month site has to order its engines and transformer before it has a tenant; the Build tab lays out every item and its window.
  7. Bringing your own power is now mainstream. Goldman projects 67 GW behind the meter by 2030. Turbines are sold out for years, so reciprocating engines and fuel cells carry the near-term load at an all-in cost of roughly $100–165 per MWh in the US.
  8. A gigawatt costs $35–60B, and most of it is chips. Crusoe's CEO puts the site and power plant near $20M per MW and the compute near $40M. NVIDIA says $50–60B, Blackstone $55B, Foxconn about $47B for Vera Rubin, Bernstein about $35B. Renting the chips earns about $15M per MW a year; adding managed inference can lift that toward $30M. The lab above them earns far more: Anthropic's S-1 shows inference revenue per megawatt rising from about $16M to over $60M in a year across its whole fleet, with revenue up 1,368% against compute cost up 300% (SemiAnalysis, 27 Oct 2026).

The value chain, from electrons to tokens

Every company in this guide sits on one or more of these layers. The further up you go, the more revenue per megawatt, the more capital per megawatt, and the shorter the contract.

CoreWeave's own unit economics: the March 2026 investor deck

CoreWeave's version of the contract calculator, from its March 2026 investor presentation. One five-year take-or-pay contract, no prepayment, a 75% contracted EBITDA margin, and an 80% loan-to-cost delayed-draw loan at 6.5% over five years. Of every dollar of contract revenue, 16 cents are left for the parent after the lease, opex, interest, principal and the parent's 20% of capex, which the deck says covers about five more years of data-center lease after the contract ends.

A new deployment's P&L, per $100 of stabilized revenue

Lease, power and non-server depreciation start on day 0 when the powered shell is handed over; GPU depreciation starts at day 21 when provisioning begins; revenue starts at day 45. Contribution margin stabilizes in the mid-20s; the long-term adjusted operating margin target is 25–30%. The second chart is the deck's illustration of one equal-sized deployment ramping every period: the aggregate margin troughs at 5% while deployment 2 starts before deployment 1 has stabilized.

What the deck says sets the price of a DDTL

Three things, in CoreWeave's order: the quality of the contract (committed multi-year reserved capacity, no termination for convenience), the customer's credit (the loan is a look-through to the customer's borrowing cost, which is how CoreWeave borrows below its own senior rate), and the operator's record of execution. Weighted average interest fell from 14.9% in 2023 to 12.2% in 2024 and 9.0% in 2025 on $21.6B of debt, about $700M a year of interest at the Q4 balance. $12.9B had been raised through DDTL 1–3 at the time of the deck; the Financing tab has DDTL 5.5 at SOFR + 550 seven months later.

What the desk makes of it

The waterfall is the honest version of the bull case and it is sensitive to one input. At the deck's 6.5% the interest line is 8% of contract revenue; at the 9.4% that DDTL 5.5 actually costs it is about 11.5%, and the parent's residual falls from 16% to roughly 12%, before any tax. The deck also makes CoreWeave's planning assumption explicit: a six-year GPU useful life against five-year contracts, with the residual value of the chips, not the contract, covering the lease tail. What a signed lease is worth: Cantor's lease-by-lease DCF

Cantor Fitzgerald (26 Oct 2026) ran a discounted cash flow on every lease seven listed landlords have signed: year-one rent, an NOI margin, the term, a WACC built from each lease's own cost of debt (cost of equity 13%), and either a terminal value at 10x NOI (a 10% cap rate) or none. The exercise answers the pushback that these buildings are worthless when the lease ends. For Kona it is the clearest public statement of how a buyer would price a Vaca Muerta lease: by the tenant's rent, discounted at the project's own debt cost plus a thin equity slice, with the building's afterlife as the swing.

Source: Cantor Fitzgerald, "Addressing DCF Pushback on AIDCs", Brett Knoblauch and Ben Mitchell, 26 Oct 2026; FactSet prices at 1:35pm that day. Capex is Cantor's year-zero figure for the lease (zero where the site was already built or tenant-funded). Cantor's WACC for Core42 is shown as 13% in the note; the sensitivity grid centers on 9.2%.

    What Kona is selling against

    Kona's asset is cheap, stranded gas turned into firm power at a site that needs no grid interconnection. Its constraints are scale (tens of megawatts while the market signs hundreds), country risk, and distance from customers.

    The six models below are scored against those facts. The view at the end is a framing for discussion with Matías and Ken. It is not investment advice, and the Kona-specific numbers are arithmetic on market data points, not Kona's own model.

        How it works

        Capacity = original deal ÷ base revenue per MW ÷ contract years. Capex = capacity × capex per MW. During the contract, revenue = price-adjusted revenue per MW × capacity; after it, the residual percentage of that revenue runs to the end of useful life. Opex = revenue × (1 − EBITDA margin). Debt = debt share × capex, repaid in equal annual amounts over the contract, with interest on the opening balance. Cash tax = max(0, EBITDA − capex ÷ useful life) × tax rate. The unlevered IRR is on capex against EBITDA less cash tax, so the debt lines show the funding and the owner's cash position but do not change the return.

        How to read it

        Delayed-draw loan

        The senior facility is committed at the advance rate on total capex and drawn alongside each construction payment, so interest runs only on what is drawn and a commitment fee runs on the rest. Interest during construction is paid by equity. After any interest-only period it amortizes level, is sculpted to a target debt service cover, or is repaid in one bullet; anything still outstanding at tenor is repaid as a balloon, which is where refinancing risk sits.

        Prepayment

        A customer prepayment is cash in during construction and a rent credit afterwards: headline rent stays the same but cash rent is lower for the contract term. It is the cheapest capital in the market and the hardest to get.

        Term mismatch

        When the contract is shorter than the loan, rent after the term is marked down to the renewal price. The GPU case reproduces CoreWeave's DDTL 5.5: three-year contracts, a five-year loan, chips re-rented at half price in years four and five.

        What it leaves out

        Taxes, Argentine withholding and remittance limits, currency, working capital, GPU refresh capex and any value of the asset at the end of the horizon unless you set a terminal multiple. Treat the outputs as a way to compare structures, not as a forecast.
